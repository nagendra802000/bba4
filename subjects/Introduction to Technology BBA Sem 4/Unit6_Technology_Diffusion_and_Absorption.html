Complete Exam Notes

1. Introduction & Learning Objectives

Modern business settings are significantly shaped by rapid technological advancement. To thrive in today's competitive world, businesses need to be innovative, adaptable and efficient.

Strategically used technology increases output, lowers expenses, improves product quality and enables quicker response to market needs.

However, success is not assured by just using new technologies. Businesses must prioritise technology adoption by cultivating knowledgeable staff, implementing helpful systems and securing strong management support.

Understanding the life cycle of technologies — from introduction to decline — allows informed investment decisions. It is critical to match technological decisions with long-term objectives.

Learning Objectives — Write in Exam
  1. Explain concepts of technology adoption, diffusion, absorption and technology life cycle in organisations.
  2. Apply knowledge of diffusion and absorption to examine how firms improve productivity and competitiveness.
  3. Analyse stages of technology life cycle and their influence on business decisions.
  4. Evaluate technological trends and design strategies for organisations to sustain innovation and avoid obsolescence.

2. Technology Adoption and Technology Diffusion

Adoption and diffusion explain acceptance and spread of new technologies. Diffusion is the gradual spread of new technology, while adoption occurs when people or organisations choose to use it.

Both are essential to guarantee that innovation produces genuine value. Early adoption can boost competitive advantage, cut expenses and increase productivity. Adoption choices are influenced by cost, compatibility, risk and user approval. Communication, societal influence and business practices also play a role in diffusion.

2.1 Technology Adoption

Meaning: Technology adoption is the process by which individuals or organisations decide to accept and use new technology in place of existing methods.

Adoption involves not only initial acceptance but also practical use in regular operations. It signifies commitment of resources and willingness to change established practices. It is a critical step in transforming innovations into practical and economic benefits.

Adoption as a Decision-Making Process

Technology adoption is not a one-time event but the result of methodical decision-making.

Finding Need / Opportunity → Assessment → Testing → Approval

Decision-makers evaluate:

  • Technical feasibility: Performance, dependability and compatibility.
  • Economic viability: Costs, returns and long-term financial effects.
  • Organisational suitability: Skills, infrastructure, managerial support and change-readiness.

Risks, alternatives and alignment with strategic objectives are also considered. This reduces uncertainty and prevents poor decisions.

Individual vs Organisational Adoption

Individual Adoption Organisational Adoption
Personal acceptance and use. Influenced by motivation, abilities, experience, perceived utility, attitudes and ease of use. Multifaceted and team-based. Includes official approvals, resource allocation, strategy alignment and several stakeholders.
When technology is easy to use and obviously helpful, adoption can be voluntary and faster. Requires departmental collaboration and frequent adjustments to responsibilities and procedures.
More directly influenced by individual perceptions. More organised and time-consuming; influenced by culture, leadership support and receptivity to change.

Stages in Technology Adoption — 5 Stages

Very Important — 5 Marks: Awareness → Interest → Evaluation → Trial → Adoption

a) Awareness

Potential adopters discover that new technology exists but know very little about it. Sources may include advertising, media, industry reports and professional networks. No decision is made at this stage. The emphasis is on acknowledgement, lowering ambiguity and preparing for further testing.

b) Interest

Prospective adopters seek comprehensive information to understand how the technology operates and its potential uses. They may consult early adopters, case studies, specialists and suppliers.

Perceptions are shaped by the trustworthiness and quality of information. Clear communication and demonstrations connect awareness with deeper analysis.

c) Evaluation

The technology is assessed in detail by comparing expected benefits against costs, risks and organisational requirements.

Factors include compatibility, economic feasibility and strategic alignment. Feasibility studies may be conducted or experts consulted. A negative assessment can halt adoption entirely.

d) Trial

The technology is tested on a limited scale. Pilot projects, prototypes and experimental use provide hands-on experience and reduce uncertainty.

Trials help identify implementation challenges, training needs and performance outcomes. Successful trials build confidence.

e) Adoption

Full acceptance and regular use occurs. The technology becomes integrated into routine operations and is supported by training, procedures and management commitment.

Factors Influencing Technology Adoption — 5 Factors

Remember: Cost → Relative Advantage → Compatibility → Complexity → Organisational Readiness

a) Cost and Economic Feasibility

High upfront costs, uncertain operating costs and unclear rewards can hamper adoption. Businesses weigh benefits such as cost savings and increased production against acquisition, installation, training and maintenance costs.

Technologies offering incentives, shorter payback periods and clear financial value are adopted faster.

b) Relative Advantage

Relative advantage is the extent to which new technology is perceived as better than existing alternatives.

Technologies providing improvements in performance, efficiency, quality, speed and flexibility are generally adopted faster.

c) Compatibility

Compatibility refers to how well new technology aligns with current systems, processes, values and practices.

High compatibility minimises disruption, reduces training needs and improves user acceptance. Technologies requiring major changes may face greater resistance.

d) Complexity

Complexity refers to the perceived difficulty of understanding and using technology. Highly complex technologies may require advanced skills, extensive training and significant learning effort.

User-friendly interfaces, training and technical support can reduce perceived complexity.

e) Organisational Readiness

Organisational readiness means the availability of skills, infrastructure, financial resources and managerial support.

Strong leadership commitment, trained personnel and a supportive culture improve readiness. Lack of preparedness can lead to delayed or failed adoption.

2.2 Technology Diffusion

Meaning: Technology diffusion is the process by which technological innovation spreads from its point of origin to a broader population of users over time.

Unlike adoption, which focuses on a single decision-maker, diffusion describes the collective pattern of acceptance and use across individuals, organisations and industries.

Diffusion as Spread Over Time

Diffusion occurs gradually rather than instantly. The rate varies depending on the nature of the technology, market conditions and user perceptions.

Early adopters accept first, followed by wider groups as uncertainty decreases and benefits become visible.

Over time, diffusion typically follows an S-shaped pattern: slow initially, rapid acceleration and finally a levelling off as the market approaches saturation.

Adoption vs Diffusion

Basis Adoption Diffusion
Micro vs Macro Micro-level — individual or organisational decision to accept and use. Macro-level — overall spread across a population or system.
Focus Decision to use. Pattern of spread.
Relationship Adoption decisions collectively shape diffusion. Diffusion influences adoption by reducing uncertainty and increasing visibility.
Time Decision point and implementation. Gradual process over time.

Role of Communication Channels

Communication channels are vital because they transmit information.

  • Formal channels: Advertising, training programmes and professional publications.
  • Informal channels: Peer interactions and word of mouth.

Effective communication reduces uncertainty and accelerates diffusion by increasing understanding and trust.

Influence of Social Systems and Networks

Social systems and networks shape attitudes, norms and behaviour. Opinion leaders, early adopters and professional networks can act as catalysts.

Trust within networks increases credibility and can speed diffusion.

Organisational and Industry-Level Diffusion

At organisational level, diffusion depends on internal communication, leadership support and learning mechanisms.

At industry level, diffusion is influenced by competition, standards, regulations and inter-firm networks.

2.3 Importance of Technology Diffusion — 6 Points

1. Enhances Productivity and Efficiency

As new technologies spread, firms gain access to improved tools, processes and methods that enable more output with the same or fewer resources.

2. Promotes Standardisation and Best Practices

Widespread diffusion encourages common technical standards, operating procedures and industry norms. This improves coordination and reduces incompatibility and transaction costs.

3. Reduces Cost Through Widespread Use

Diffusion reduces costs through economies of scale and learning effects. Increased production volumes can lower unit costs while supplier experience improves technology.

4. Improves Competitiveness of Firms and Industries

Diffusion gives firms access to modern technologies that improve efficiency, quality and innovation capability.

5. Supports Economic and Industrial Development

At macro level, technology diffusion supports economic growth by improving productivity and fostering industrial development.

6. Facilitates Learning, Imitation and Incremental Innovation

Diffusion encourages learning and imitation, allowing firms to adapt and improve existing technologies. Minor improvements can accumulate over time.

3. Innovation Diffusion Process

Innovation diffusion explains how innovative concepts or technologies proliferate over time within a social system.

It goes through several phases:

Knowledge → Persuasion → Decision → Implementation → Confirmation

Time, social impact, communication channels and the type of innovation affect the speed and effectiveness of diffusion.

3.1 Different Perspectives of Innovation Diffusion

1. Economic Perspective

The economic perspective focuses on costs, benefits and anticipated returns. When anticipated financial benefits outweigh costs such as investment, training, operations and changeover, people and organisations are more likely to embrace new technology.

Early adopters take greater risk and expense in exchange for potential first-mover benefits. Learning effects and economies of scale lower costs and uncertainty as acceptance rises.

2. Sociological Perspective

The sociological perspective describes diffusion through social influence, communication and relationships.

Adoption choices are influenced by social norms, peer pressure, shared beliefs, others' experiences, professional networks and opinion leaders.

3. Organisational Perspective

The organisational perspective focuses on internal factors such as structure, culture, leadership and strategy.

Flexible structures and supportive cultures promote experimentation and faster diffusion, while rigid hierarchies can hinder adoption.

4. System-Level Perspective

Diffusion is viewed as part of a larger socio-economic system involving institutions, policies and networks.

Regulations, standards, intellectual property and policies affect diffusion. Innovation systems include firms, universities, research institutions, financial entities and intermediaries.

3.2 Technology Diffusion Curve — S-Shaped Curve

Most Important: Technology diffusion normally follows an S-shaped pattern — slow initially, rapid growth in the middle and slowing as saturation is reached.

Concept of Diffusion Over Time

Technology spreads gradually because individuals and organisations differ in awareness, risk tolerance, resources and readiness.

Initially diffusion is slow due to uncertainty and limited information. As experience accumulates and benefits become visible, adoption accelerates. Eventually it slows when most potential adopters have adopted.

S-Shaped Diffusion Curve

At first adoption is sluggish because the technology is new, unknown, risky and costly. Only curious and eager users may try it.

Growth occurs as early adopters embrace the technology, awareness rises and positive experiences are shared.

When the early majority begins adopting, expansion becomes rapid because benefits are more obvious, ambiguity is reduced, expenses fall and infrastructure improves.

Finally, adoption slows as the market approaches saturation. Laggards may adopt last or continue resisting because of tradition, risk aversion or lack of resources.

Rate of Adoption Across the Population

a) Variation in Adoption Rates

Adoption rates vary because users differ in resources, perceived advantages, attitudes toward change and readiness.

b) Early Adoption Behaviour

Early adopters may be motivated by curiosity, inventiveness and the desire for tactical or competitive benefits.

They often possess financial resources, technical knowledge and management support and are willing to accept more risk and uncertainty.

c) Late Adoption Behaviour

Late adopters wait until technology becomes reliable, reasonably priced and widely used.

They may be risk-averse and prefer established norms and proven performance.

d) Factors Influencing Adoption Speed

  • Risk perception
  • Economic capability
  • Technical skills and skilled personnel
  • Organisational culture
  • Leadership
  • Government policies
  • Industry competition
  • Infrastructure availability
  • Social influence and peer pressure

e) Managerial Implications

Managers can use adoption patterns to predict market growth and identify target segments for adopter strategies.

This supports planning for launches, pricing and promotions, resource allocation, training and support systems.

Managerial Implications for Adopter Categories

Targeted market-entry strategy should align with adopter groups.

  • Early phase: Focus on innovators and early adopters using demonstrations, pilot projects and performance validation.
  • Early and late majority: Focus on scaling adoption, standardisation, simplicity, economic rewards and dependability.
  • Communication should increasingly emphasise demonstrated value and competitive pricing.
  • Resource allocation shifts from R&D and learning towards operational efficiency, customer service and cost management.

4. Technology Absorption

Meaning: Technology absorption is the capacity of an organisation to recognise, acquire, understand, adapt to and successfully use new technologies to enhance performance.

Absorption focuses on learning from technology and turning knowledge into practical applications. It goes beyond simple adoption.

Technology absorption is influenced by prior knowledge, knowledgeable staff, learning capacity, supportive culture, training and information-sharing systems.

Integration with current procedures and management commitment are essential. Inadequate absorption can result in underutilised technology and low ROI.

4.1 Meaning of Technology Absorption

1. Concept

Technology absorption is the ability to effectively acquire, understand, adapt to and utilise new technology in operations.

It goes beyond purchasing or introducing technology and focuses on how well it is internalised and integrated into processes.

Adoption vs Absorption

Adoption Absorption
Decision to accept and implement new technology. Depth and effectiveness of its use.
Initial decision-making stage. Continuous process that follows adoption.
Organisation may adopt but fail to use effectively. Ensures technology contributes meaningfully to performance.
Example: Buying a robot. Example: Training, customising, integrating and improving the robot daily.
Key Point: An organisation may adopt technology but fail to absorb it because of lack of skills, inadequate processes or resistance to change.

Absorptive Capacity

Absorptive capacity is the ability to recognise the value of new technology, assimilate it and apply it effectively.

It depends on prior knowledge, technical expertise, training systems and organisational learning mechanisms.

Firms with higher absorptive capacity can adapt technologies more efficiently and use them as a basis for further innovation.

Importance of Learning and Adaptation

Continuous training, experimentation and feedback help refine technology usage. Adaptation allows technology to fit organisational needs rather than forcing rigid application.

Effective absorption transforms technology into a strategic resource that enhances competitiveness and long-term performance.

4.2 Process of Technology Absorption — 4 Stages

Acquisition → Assimilation → Adaptation → Effective Utilisation & Improvement

1. Acquisition of Technology

The process begins by identifying appropriate technologies and obtaining them through purchase, licensing, collaboration, joint ventures or technology transfer.

Organisations evaluate relevance, cost, compatibility and strategic fit. Acquisition also includes technical knowledge, documentation and expertise.

2. Assimilation of Knowledge

Assimilation involves understanding and internalising acquired technology. It includes employee training, technical skill development and building internal knowledge bases.

Documentation, workshops and learning-by-doing transfer external knowledge into organisational routines.

3. Adaptation to Identified Needs

Adaptation involves modifying and customising technology to meet specific organisational needs.

This may include changes in processes, software customisation and integration with existing systems.

4. Effective Utilisation and Improvement

The final stage focuses on effective day-to-day utilisation and continuous improvement.

Feedback, monitoring and incremental modifications optimise usage over time. Absorbed technology can then become a platform for further innovation and sustained competitiveness.

4.3 Benefits of Technology Absorption — 5 Benefits

1. Improved Productivity and Efficiency

Tasks can be completed more quickly and correctly when employees fully understand and use technology.

Errors, waste and resource consumption can decrease while workflow coordination, teamwork and consistent quality improve.

2. Development of Technical Capabilities

Employees gain new skills through education, training and practical experience. Internal knowledge reduces reliance on outside assistance.

3. Reduced Dependence on External Sources

Strong internal knowledge reduces reliance on external vendors, consultants and suppliers.

Organisations can address problems, improve systems and implement technologies more independently.

4. Enhanced Innovation Potential

A strong technological foundation allows organisations to experiment, recombine knowledge and discover new applications.

Strong absorption skills can support both incremental improvements and significant innovations.

5. Long-Term Competitive Advantage

Organisations with stronger absorptive capacity can achieve better results through faster learning, innovation and responsiveness.

Knowledge accumulated over time becomes an internal asset that can be difficult to replicate.

5. Issues and Constraints in Technology Absorption

Organisations may struggle to acquire, learn and integrate new technologies. Firms may not fully benefit from heavy investment because of weak absorption capacity.

Common challenges include skilled personnel shortages, limited expertise, resistance to change, poor learning cultures, inadequate training, infrastructure constraints, funding shortages and compatibility problems.

5.1 Constraints in Technology Absorption — 5 Constraints

1. Lack of Skilled Manpower

Effective absorption requires employees with technical knowledge, problem-solving ability and a learning orientation.

Skill gaps slow learning, increase errors and limit the ability to adapt and improve technology.

2. Inadequate Infrastructure

Modern technology may require reliable power, advanced equipment, IT systems and maintenance facilities.

Poor infrastructure reduces efficiency, increases downtime and discourages further investment.

3. Financial Constraints

Acquisition, training, customisation and maintenance can involve high costs. SMEs may adopt technology but fail to allocate sufficient resources for effective absorption.

4. Resistance to Change

Employees and management may resist because of fear of job loss, increased workload or unfamiliar systems.

Resistance slows absorption and prevents organisations from realising the full benefits of technology.

5. Weak R&D and Learning Systems

Without structured training, knowledge sharing and continuous learning mechanisms, organisations may struggle to internalise and improve technologies.

5.2 Issues in Management of Technology Absorption — 5 Issues

1. Integration with Existing Systems

New technology may have to be integrated with legacy systems. Technical mismatches, data integration problems and process disruptions can reduce efficiency and delay benefits.

2. Training and Skill Development Challenges

Limited training budgets, time constraints and uneven skill levels can hinder learning.

3. Knowledge Transfer Difficulties

Transferring knowledge from external sources to internal users can be difficult, especially when the knowledge is tacit and difficult to document.

Poor documentation and weak communication can leave organisations dependent on external experts.

4. Organisational and Cultural Barriers

Resistance to change, rigid hierarchies and lack of collaboration can discourage learning and experimentation.

5. Protection and Effective Use of Absorbed Technology

Organisations must protect intellectual assets while ensuring effective utilisation and continuous improvement.

Weak control systems may result in knowledge leakage or misuse.

5.3 Government Initiatives for Technology Absorption — 5 Initiatives

1. Policy and Regulatory Support

Governments can support technology absorption by establishing clear and consistent policies that reduce uncertainty and promote investment.

Clear regulations and technical standards improve compatibility, safety and quality. Tax incentives, grants, subsidies and technology-transfer programmes can reduce financial risk.

Examples

  • India — UPI Digital Payments: Clear rules, collaboration with RBI, standardised platforms, interoperability and data-security guidelines facilitated adoption.
  • Pharma IP Protection: Strong patent laws encourage R&D, advanced manufacturing and global collaboration.
  • Renewable Energy Industry: Subsidies, tax breaks and renewable energy mandates encourage solar and wind technology.
  • IT Industry: Business-friendly policies, software export incentives and secure data frameworks support adoption of cloud and automation technologies.

2. Financial Incentives and Subsidies

Governments may provide grants, subsidies, tax breaks and low-interest loans to reduce the financial burden of technology adoption.

SMEs may particularly benefit because they often have fewer financial resources. Support can help fund training, process reform and modern systems.

3. Support for R&D and Technology Transfer

Governments can support R&D infrastructure and facilitate technology transfer from academic institutions to enterprises.

Universities, research institutions, innovation centres and technology parks can provide access to expertise, resources and information.

4. Schemes for SMEs and Startups

Government programmes can provide incubation, mentorship and shared facilities.

Incubation centres provide space, support and networking for experimentation. Shared laboratories, testing centres and digital platforms can reduce costs and improve learning.

5. Collaboration with Academic and Research Institutions

Academic institutions provide scientific knowledge, skilled personnel and technical expertise.

Partnerships through sponsored research, consulting and joint projects promote learning, skill development and knowledge transfer.

Internships, training and industry-aligned curricula can improve the workforce's ability to understand, adapt and use technology.

6. Technology Life Cycle (TLC)

Definition: The Technology Life Cycle outlines the evolution of a technology from development to obsolescence.

Technologies generally start as experimental, expensive and restricted. Adoption increases, costs decline and standardisation rises as maturity increases.

During maturity, improvements slow, competition increases and firms focus on efficiency and incremental innovation.

Eventually, new technologies may replace existing technologies, causing decline.

Understanding the technology life cycle helps companies plan investments, allocate resources and manage the timing of adoption and innovation.

6.1 Concept of Technology Life Cycle

The Technology Life Cycle describes the phases technology passes through from initial development to eventual decline or replacement.

Usually, the cycle consists of:

Introduction

Novel, expensive, unpredictable and not widely used.

Growth

Improvements boost adoption and reduce expenses.

Maturity

Widespread usage, standardisation and minor improvements.

Decline

Technology becomes less relevant as better alternatives emerge.

Evolution of Technology Over Time

Technology evolves through continuous learning, testing and creativity. Initial versions are often flawed and require improvement.

User feedback and technological advances improve performance and reduce costs. As technology matures, innovation moves from radical changes to minor modifications.

Eventually, technological limits may be reached and further improvements produce diminishing returns. New technologies may then replace existing technologies.

6.2 Stages of Technology Life Cycle — 4 Stages

Most Important — 10 Marks: Emergence → Growth → Maturity → Saturation & Decline

a) Emergence / Introduction Stage

The life cycle begins when a technology is first created and released. There is significant uncertainty, high pricing and poor initial performance.

Research, testing and technological improvement are prioritised over broad commercial use.

Consumers may not be familiar with the technology and complementary assets, skilled labour and supporting infrastructure may be underdeveloped.

Early adopters may seek to protect intellectual property, develop skills and gain first-mover advantage.

b) Growth Stage

The growth stage is characterised by rapid performance improvements and rising market adoption.

Experience and learning effects reduce costs and improve reliability. Businesses invest in increasing output, improving processes and building supporting resources such as distribution networks and services.

As more firms enter, standards become established and competition increases.

Exam Point: The growth stage offers strong opportunities for market leadership, revenue growth and economies of scale.

c) Maturity Stage

The technology reaches high performance and widespread use. Improvements become mostly incremental as technological limits are approached.

Market growth slows and competition intensifies. Firms focus on cost efficiency, quality, process optimisation and operational excellence.

Standardisation increases, making differentiation more difficult.

The maturity stage also indicates the need to explore new technologies for future growth.

d) Saturation and Decline Stage

Technology reaches its peak and there is little room for further improvement. Demand may stabilise or decline because of changing preferences, obsolescence or better alternatives.

Firms may face shrinking margins, surplus capacity and strong price competition.

Organisations may reduce investment, focus on niche markets or redirect resources toward new technologies.

6.3 Strategic Implications of Technology Life Cycle — 4 Implications

1. Technology Investment Decisions

The life-cycle stage strongly influences investment decisions.

  • Introduction: Careful investment in research, experimentation and pilot projects.
  • Growth: Increase investment to expand capacity and improve processes.
  • Maturity: Focus on efficiency, cost reduction and minor enhancements.
  • Decline: Reduce or withdraw investment and manage existing assets carefully.

2. Timing of Innovation and Diffusion

The technology life cycle indicates when to innovate and when to diffuse technology.

Early entry involves greater risk but may provide first-mover advantages, technology leadership and influence over industry standards.

Later entry lowers risk but may reduce competitive advantages.

During growth, rapid diffusion can drive economies of scale and market growth. During maturity, firms increasingly focus on protecting market share.

3. Technology Upgrading or Replacement

Organisations must decide whether to upgrade existing technology or replace it.

During growth and early maturity, upgrades can improve performance and extend usefulness.

During decline, incremental improvements may produce lower returns. Replacement becomes necessary when new technologies offer better performance, lower costs or improved market fit.

4. Planning for Next-Generation Technologies

Organisations should plan for future technologies before current technologies become obsolete.

Proactive planning includes monitoring new technologies, investing in R&D and developing skills and knowledge.

During maturity, forward-thinking organisations can experiment with next-generation solutions to support future transitions.

8. Glossary — 15 Definitions

  1. Technology Life Cycle (TLC): Pattern showing how technology develops over time, from introduction and experimentation to widespread use and eventually decline or replacement.
  2. Innovation Stage: Early phase where new technology is invented, tested and refined, often involving high uncertainty and heavy R&D investment.
  3. Growth Stage: Period when technology gains market acceptance, production expands, costs reduce and demand rises quickly.
  4. Maturity Stage: Stable phase where technology is widely used, improvements are mostly minor and competition focuses on efficiency and cost reduction.
  5. Decline Stage: Phase in which technology loses relevance because superior or cheaper alternatives emerge.
  6. Diffusion of Technology: Process through which technology spreads across individuals, firms, industries or countries over time.
  7. Obsolescence: Condition in which technology becomes outdated or less valuable due to newer innovations or changing market needs.
  8. R&D: Organised efforts by firms or institutions to create new technologies or improve existing ones.
  9. Technology Adoption: Decision and process by which individuals or organisations begin to use and integrate new technology into operations.
  10. Competitive Advantage: Strategic benefit achieved when firms use technology more effectively than rivals, improving productivity, cost or product quality.
  11. Process Innovation: Improvements in production, delivery or operational methods through application of new or improved technology.
  12. Product Innovation: Creation of new products or significant enhancement of existing products using technological advancements.
  13. Technology Substitution: Replacement of existing technology with a more efficient, cost-effective or advanced alternative.
  14. Strategic Planning: Long-term decision-making that considers technological trends, risks and life-cycle stages to guide organisational growth.
  15. Technological Forecasting: Systematic prediction of future technological developments to help firms prepare investments, innovations and strategies.

9. Case Study — Alpha Manufacturing

Case: Alpha Manufacturing is a medium-sized manufacturer of equipment that had long relied on traditional manufacturing methods.

Production costs increased and delivery times lengthened as competitors began using digital monitoring systems and automation.

Alpha decided to import cutting-edge automated machinery to maintain its competitive position.

Employees initially had difficulty learning the new method, causing production to decline.

Upper management then funded staff education, process changes and regional technical assistance.

Productivity increased, failure rates decreased and Alpha recovered market share within two years.

Alpha must now decide whether to invest in AI-powered predictive maintenance systems to avoid future obsolescence.

Q1. Stages of Technology Life Cycle Reflected

  • Traditional methods: Maturity/Decline stage — high costs, longer delivery times and loss of competitiveness.
  • Importing automated machinery: Emergence/Growth stage — new technology, high uncertainty, learning requirements and initial production decline.
  • After training and process change: Growth stage — productivity increased, failures decreased and market share recovered.
  • Considering AI predictive maintenance: Need to plan for the next S-curve and next-generation technology before current automation reaches maturity or decline.

Q2. How Technology Absorption Helped

Alpha used the four-stage absorption process:

  1. Acquisition: Imported automated machinery.
  2. Assimilation: Staff education, training, regional technical assistance and development of internal knowledge.
  3. Adaptation: Process changes were made to suit the new technology.
  4. Effective Utilisation: Productivity increased, failure rates decreased and market share recovered.
Key Lesson: Adoption alone is not enough. Organisations also need absorptive capacity, training, learning and management support.

Q3. Strategic Steps to Avoid Obsolescence

  1. Continuous technology scanning and forecasting: Monitor AI, IoT trends, patents and competitors.
  2. Technology roadmapping: Create a visual timeline for upgrading automation toward AI predictive maintenance.
  3. Invest in R&D and learning systems: Build absorptive capacity for AI and avoid skill gaps.
  4. Pilot testing: Test AI predictive maintenance on one production line before full-scale implementation.
  5. Collaboration: Work with academic institutions, technology parks and vendors for knowledge transfer.
  6. Financial planning: Allocate budgets for next-generation technology while current technology is still in growth or maturity.
  7. S-curve analysis: Identify when current automation reaches saturation and determine when replacement should occur.
  8. Government initiatives: Use subsidies, training programmes and tax incentives where applicable.
Conclusion: Proactive planning is required rather than reactive planning to sustain competitiveness and avoid technological obsolescence.
