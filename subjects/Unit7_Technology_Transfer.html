1. Introduction & Learning Objectives

Technology transfer is vital for economic and industrial growth because it enables businesses to adopt advanced knowledge, skills and processes from other sources.

It reduces the need for expensive and lengthy R&D by allowing companies to adapt existing technologies to their own needs.

Effective technology transfer can boost innovation, enhance productivity, improve product quality and help firms remain competitive in local and global markets.

Technology transfer involves more than simply transferring equipment. It encompasses technical knowledge, management practices, training, documentation and support required for proper use.

Successful transfer depends on skilled staff, organisational readiness, funding and supportive policies.

Technology transfer can occur through licensing, joint ventures, foreign investment, consulting and collaborative research, often supported by governments and institutions.

Learning Objectives — Write in Exam
  1. Explain concepts, process and significance of technology transfer in organisational and economic development.
  2. Apply different modes, routes and models of technology transfer to real industrial or institutional situations.
  3. Analyse issues, dimensions and challenges affecting effective technology transfer and absorption.
  4. Evaluate and design appropriate governance mechanisms, agreements and strategic approaches to ensure successful outcomes.

2. Technology Transfer — Meaning, Objectives, Process & Features

2.1 Meaning and Definition of Technology Transfer

Definition: Technology transfer is the process by which technology moves between organisations, institutions or countries for practical use.

Technology transfer includes physical equipment as well as intangible elements such as technical knowledge, skills, production methods, procedures and managerial practices.

Successful transfer requires the recipient to learn, adapt and integrate technology into local conditions, workforce capabilities and available resources.

Absorption and improvement can increase productivity, quality and innovation, strengthen competitiveness and economic growth, and promote long-term technological self-reliance.

Objectives and Significance — 9 Points

Must Write for 5 Marks
  1. Give nations and organisations access to cutting-edge, tested technology without the time, expense and risks of independent R&D.
  2. Provide a quicker and more affordable path to technological development by using existing technology.
  3. Encourage industrial progress by bridging the technology gap between industrialised and less-developed nations.
  4. Increase production through contemporary equipment, practical procedures and improved management techniques.
  5. Increase product quality, lower manufacturing costs and improve reliability.
  6. Facilitate domestic manufacturing of previously imported products, reducing dependence on imports.
  7. Enhance technical and management skills through education, training and skill development.
  8. Encourage the use of optimal technology and efficient utilisation of resources.
  9. Support long-term technical independence, competitiveness and innovation.

Role in Innovation and Industrial Development

Technology transfer promotes innovation and industrial growth by allowing companies to strengthen technical expertise and internal capabilities.

Instead of simply replicating technologies, businesses can adapt them to local circumstances, market demands and available resources.

At the industrial level, technology transfer improves competitiveness by increasing productivity, quality and efficiency, modernising manufacturing and enabling diversification into new sectors.

Over time, it can reduce technological dependence by promoting skill development, employment generation and sustainable economic growth.

2.2 Technology Transfer Process — 6 Stages

Very Important — 10 Marks: Need Identification → Search & Selection → Feasibility → Negotiation → Implementation/Absorption/Adaptation → Learning & Improvement

1. Identification of Technology Need

This is the first and crucial step. The organisation evaluates its current technological capabilities against its objectives.

Technology needs may arise from low productivity, outdated processes, quality issues, rising costs, competition, regulations or market changes.

Management must specify the required product, process or managerial technology and ensure alignment with long-term strategy, financial capacity and operational goals.

2. Technology Search and Selection

Organisations identify potential sources of technology. Sources may be domestic or international and include technology suppliers, research institutions, universities, MNCs and licensing agencies.

Technologies are evaluated based on maturity, reliability, compatibility, availability of technical support and supplier credibility.

3. Evaluation of Technical and Economic Feasibility

Technical feasibility examines whether the technology can work with existing production systems, infrastructure and workforce skills.

Economic feasibility includes acquisition, installation, operating and training costs along with expected returns, payback period, profitability and long-term sustainability.

Key idea: A technology must be technically practical and economically viable before adoption or agreement.

4. Negotiation and Finalisation

The technology provider and recipient establish mutually beneficial terms.

Important matters include:

  • Licensing fees
  • Royalties
  • Duration
  • Scope of use
  • Technical support
  • Training
  • Intellectual property rights

Legal, financial and technical experts may participate to prevent ambiguity and future disputes.

5. Implementation, Absorption and Adaptation

Implementation involves installing and using the technology. Absorption is the capacity to understand, use and manage it effectively.

Employees may require training, while workflows and organisational structures may need adjustment.

Adaptation modifies the technology to fit local materials, skills, climate, regulations and market requirements.

Poor absorption can result in underutilisation.

6. Learning and Improvement After Transfer

This is the final and ongoing stage. Hands-on experience helps organisations understand the technology better and identify opportunities for improvement and innovation.

Continuous learning can improve efficiency, reduce costs, improve quality, build internal capabilities and reduce dependence on external sources.

2.3 Features of Technology Transfer — 5 Components

A technology transfer package includes more than machines. It brings together five major components.

1. Hardware

Machinery, tools and equipment. Selection, installation, calibration and maintenance are important.

2. Software & Know-how

Methods, procedures, controls, problem-solving skills, computer programmes and systems.

3. Managerial Practices

Management systems, workflows, organisational structures, decision-making and performance controls.

4. Training & Skill Development

Classroom instruction, on-the-job training, workshops and international exposure.

5. Documentation & Technical Support

Manuals, drawings, specifications, procedures, maintenance guides and supplier technical support.

3. Issues and Dimensions of Technology Transfer

Many obstacles can affect technology transfer, including cultural hurdles, resistance to change, excessive expenses, unclear returns, poor management, technical incompatibility and legal or intellectual property conflicts.

Major dimensions include organisational structures, human resources, legal frameworks, technological characteristics and economic variables.

3.1 Issues in Technology Transfer — 5 Issues

1. Technical Issues

Technical problems arise when transferred technology is incompatible with existing infrastructure, systems or local circumstances.

Underutilisation, malfunctions and delays may result from inadequate knowledge, poor documentation, lack of local assistance, skill shortages and difficulty obtaining replacement parts.

Differences in raw materials, environment, power supply and manufacturing scale may also reduce effectiveness.

2. Economic and Financial Issues

Technology acquisition and implementation may involve licensing fees, royalties, installation, training and maintenance costs.

Limited financial resources, restricted access to credit, exchange-rate fluctuations and uncertain returns can increase risk.

Thorough economic assessment and long-term financial planning are required.

3. Organisational and Managerial Issues

Problems occur when existing structures and practices do not align with the new technology.

Rigid systems, poor coordination, lack of vision, weak leadership and inadequate project management can cause delays, cost overruns and failure.

Management commitment, planning and coordination are essential.

4. Human Resource and Cultural Issues

Employees may resist because of fear of job loss, increased workload or uncertainty about advanced systems.

Training gaps can limit absorption. Cultural differences between providers and recipients can create communication barriers and misunderstandings, particularly in international transfers.

5. Legal and Intellectual Property Issues

These involve ownership rights, licensing conditions and protection of proprietary knowledge.

Restrictions on use, modification and resale may limit flexibility. Patent disputes, confidentiality clauses and contract terms can create legal complications.

Clear contracts, legal compliance and proper enforcement of intellectual property rights are essential.

3.3 Challenges in Effective Technology Absorption — 4 Challenges

1. Technology Dependence

Excessive dependence on external suppliers can limit an organisation's ability to innovate, adapt and modify technology independently.

Developing internal skills through training, hands-on learning and knowledge transfer reduces dependence and promotes technological independence.

2. Inadequate Infrastructure

Poor infrastructure can hinder absorption, particularly in developing economies.

Modern technologies may require dependable power, transport, communication, production systems, maintenance and spare parts.

3. Skill Gaps

Employees may lack the knowledge and skills required to use, maintain and improve new technologies.

Systematic upskilling, training, mentoring and knowledge sharing are required to build capability.

4. Adaptation Problems

Problems arise when technology is used without modification to local materials, climate, infrastructure, regulations or market demands.

Practical experience, experimentation and feedback help align technology with local needs.

4. Models of Technology Transfer

Models provide conceptual frameworks for understanding how technology moves from its source of creation to practical use.

They explain the processes, actors, interactions and mechanisms involved in transferring technical knowledge, skills and innovations.

4.1 Concept and Importance of Models

Need for Models

Models provide frameworks for managing the complex process between entities. They cover technical, economic, organisational and human factors.

Models help organisations identify, select, absorb and improve technology. They also help policymakers plan, monitor and evaluate technology-transfer initiatives.

Understanding Transfer Mechanisms

Important mechanisms include licensing, joint ventures, strategic alliances, technical collaborations and FDI.

Models show how learning, adaptation, communication, training and feedback occur during transfer.

4.2 Traditional Technology Transfer Models

Traditional Models — 3: Linear Model → Technology Push Model → Market Pull Model

1. Linear Model

Basic Scientific Research → Applied Research & Development → Production → Diffusion → Market / End Users

The Linear Model presents transfer as a simple, sequential and unidirectional process.

Knowledge is assumed to flow from research institutions and laboratories to industrial organisations and finally to consumers.

The model assumes scientific research is a primary driver of innovation.

Drawbacks: It ignores actor interactions, user input, iterative learning, adaptation, teamwork and ongoing change.

2. Technology Push Model

The Technology Push Model is a variant of the Linear Model that emphasises technological innovation as the primary driving force.

New technologies originate from scientific research, laboratory experiments and breakthroughs.

Organisations invest heavily in R&D and introduce technologies into the market. Applications and customers are searched for after the technology has been developed.

This model is common in high-technology industries such as electronics, biotechnology, pharmaceuticals and aerospace.

Drawbacks: It can ignore user preparedness, costs, market circumstances and consumer preferences. A technically advanced technology may fail commercially if user needs and adaptability are overlooked.

3. Market Pull Model

The Market Pull Model places market demand and user needs at the centre of technology development and transfer.

Development is initiated in response to customer requirements, market opportunities and competitive pressures.

Firms analyse market signals, customer feedback and unmet needs to guide R&D and technology-acquisition decisions.

The model can increase adoption and commercial success by focusing on practical problem-solving and customer needs.

Limitation: Excessive reliance on market pull may discourage radical innovation and game-changing breakthroughs.

Limitations of Traditional Models — 4 Points

  1. Disregard interaction, feedback and ongoing learning between academics, businesses, consumers and institutions.
  2. Overemphasise technical issues while overlooking organisational, interpersonal, cultural and institutional elements.
  3. Fail to capture the dynamic and iterative character of modern innovation.
  4. Provide only a partial picture, leading to the development of interactive and systems-oriented approaches.

4.3 Qualitative Technology Transfer Models

Modern qualitative models treat technology transfer as a two-way and iterative process.

1. Interactive Model

The Interactive Model views transfer as a two-way, dynamic process involving continuous communication between users, transfer agencies and technology creators.

User input influences adaptation and future development. Networks, partnerships and joint ventures enable continuous knowledge and skill sharing.

2. Systems Approach

The Systems Approach views technology transfer as part of a larger socioeconomic and institutional framework.

It considers relationships among businesses, research institutes, government organisations, financial institutions and markets.

Technical, economic, organisational, human and legal subsystems must be aligned.

3. Feedback-Based Models

Feedback-Based Models emphasise continuous learning and development. Transfer is viewed as a cycle of observation, assessment and modification.

User, operator and market feedback can identify technical problems, operational inefficiencies and local misalignments.

4. Learning-Oriented Transfer

Learning-Oriented Transfer emphasises the development of long-term technical capabilities rather than simple acquisition.

Organisations build absorptive ability through experimentation, training, problem-solving and continuous improvement.

Organisations can gradually move from passive consumers to active innovators, reducing dependence on external suppliers.

4.4 Other Technology Transfer Models

1. Network Model

The Network Model highlights linked networks of businesses, academic institutions, research facilities, suppliers, consumers, financial institutions and government organisations.

Formal partnerships and cooperative research, as well as informal exchanges such as industrial clusters and professional relationships, facilitate transfer.

2. Triple Helix Model

UNIVERSITY ↔ INDUSTRY ↔ GOVERNMENT

The Triple Helix Model explains technology transfer through dynamic interactions among government, business and academic institutions.

  • Government: Funding, regulatory frameworks and policy support.
  • Business: Converts research into commercial goods and services.
  • Universities: Produce knowledge and trained individuals.

3. Open Innovation Model

Open Innovation uses both internal and external information for technology development and transfer.

Businesses can collaborate with academic institutions, research centres, startups, suppliers, consumers and even competitors.

Technology can enter through external sources and leave through outward commercialisation of internal inventions.

4. International Technology Transfer Models

These models examine technology flows between countries, particularly between developed and developing countries.

Cultural, legal, institutional and capacity gaps can complicate transfer. Common mechanisms include FDI, licensing, joint ventures and strategic alliances.

5. Modes, Routes and Governance of Technology Transfer

Modes describe different ways technology is exchanged. Routes refer to the pathways through which technology flows. Governance focuses on rules, agreements, policies and ethical frameworks that regulate the process.

5.1 Technology Transfer Modes — 6 Modes

Most Important — 10 Marks

1. Licensing

Licensing occurs when the owner or licensor grants another party, the licensee, the right to use technology under agreed terms.

The technology may include patents, designs, trademarks, technical know-how and process knowledge.

The licensee normally pays a fee or royalty.

Licensing provides access to proven technology without requiring heavy independent R&D investment.

Agreements may specify scope, duration, territorial limits and confidentiality.

Concerns include dependence on the licensor and limited learning if know-how is restricted.

2. Joint Ventures

Two or more parties create a new entity by sharing ownership, resources, risks and rewards.

A foreign partner may contribute technology, expertise and management practices, while a local partner contributes market knowledge, infrastructure and regulatory support.

Joint ventures promote learning, adaptation and capability building through continuous interaction.

3. Strategic Alliances

Strategic alliances are cooperative agreements between independent firms that share technology, knowledge and resources without forming a new legal entity.

Activities may include technology sharing, co-development, research collaboration and marketing partnerships.

They provide flexibility and access to complementary strengths.

4. Foreign Direct Investment (FDI)

FDI occurs when a firm invests directly in production or business operations in another country.

Transfer can occur through wholly owned subsidiaries or majority ownership. It can bring advanced technology, management practices and organisational systems.

FDI also promotes skill development, employment generation and industrial growth.

5. Turnkey Projects

In a turnkey project, the technology provider designs, builds, installs and commissions a complete facility and hands it over in operational condition.

It is common in infrastructure, power plants and heavy industries.

It enables quick implementation and can reduce technical risk, but learning and internal capability development may be limited if training and knowledge transfer are inadequate.

6. Consultancy and Technical Services

Technology is transferred through advisory services, training, troubleshooting and technical assistance.

Consultants provide specialised knowledge without transferring ownership. This mode can support process improvement, capacity building and incremental innovation.

5.2 Routes / Channels of Technology Transfer

1. Market-Based Channels

Market-based channels operate through commercial transactions where technology is exchanged for economic value.

Examples include licensing, patent sales, FDI, joint ventures, strategic alliances and turnkey projects.

These channels are based on contracts, prices and ownership rights and provide legal clarity and financial incentives.

2. Non-Market Channels

Non-market channels do not involve direct commercial transactions.

Examples include government programmes, public research institutions, international aid, technical cooperation, training and knowledge-sharing initiatives.

These channels are particularly important for small firms, developing countries and emerging sectors.

4. Role of MNCs and Research Institutions

MNCs are major channels through FDI, subsidiaries, joint ventures and global supply chains. They can transfer advanced technologies, management practices and organisational systems.

Research institutions generate knowledge, develop technologies and facilitate collaboration between industry and government.

5. Role of Universities and R&D Laboratories

Universities and R&D laboratories create knowledge, develop innovations and train skilled workers.

They support technology transfer through patents, spin-off companies, consultancy and industry collaboration.

5.3 Code of Conduct for Technology Transfer — 5 Points

1. Need for Regulation

Regulation is important because bargaining power may be unequal between providers and recipients, particularly in international transfers.

Regulatory frameworks promote transparency, fairness and accountability while protecting against unfavourable terms and misuse of intellectual property.

2. Fair Pricing and Royalty Norms

Excessive fees and royalty rates can create financial burdens. Fair pricing should balance the interests of both parties and reflect technology value, performance and market conditions.

3. Avoidance of Restrictive Clauses

Restrictive clauses may limit the recipient's ability to use, modify or improve technology.

Flexible agreements support learning, adaptation, innovation and technological self-reliance.

4. Protection of National Interest

Governments may ensure that technology-transfer agreements contribute to economic growth, employment generation and capability building.

Measures may include local content requirements, skill development and absorption provisions.

5. Ethical and Sustainable Practices

Technology transfer should comply with environmental standards, safety norms and social responsibility.

Ethical conduct builds trust and supports long-term partnerships.

6. Government Support and Technology Transfer Agreements

6.1 Government Initiatives for Technology Transfer

1. Role of Government in Technology Diffusion

Government acts as a facilitator, regulator and catalyst by creating supportive policy and institutional environments.

It promotes industry-research collaboration, removes barriers to adoption, establishes regulations and standards and provides incentives.

2. Public R&D Institutions

Public R&D institutions conduct basic and applied research relevant to national priorities.

They can provide technical support through consultancy, licensing and collaborative projects and can help adapt imported technologies to local conditions.

3. Technology Parks and Incubators

Technology parks and incubators provide infrastructure and support services that promote innovation and technology transfer.

Incubators may provide mentoring, technical assistance and access to networks, helping commercialise research outputs.

4. Policy Incentives and Funding Support

Governments may provide tax benefits, subsidies, grants and soft loans. Such support reduces financial risk and encourages investment in advanced technologies.

5. Support for MSMEs and Startups

Targeted support may include financial assistance, training, infrastructure and access to technology.

Such measures can strengthen technological capabilities, innovation and employment generation.

6.2 Technology Transfer Agreement — 6 Points

a) Meaning and Importance

Definition: A Technology Transfer Agreement is a legal document regulating the transfer of technology, knowledge, patents or processes from one party to another.

It specifies terms, conditions and scope of use, providing legal certainty and safeguarding both parties' interests.

It can also define responsibilities for training, technical support and performance standards.

b) Types of Agreements — 5 Types

  1. Licensing Agreements: Right to use specific technology for royalties or fees.
  2. Technical Collaboration Agreements: Know-how, training and technical support.
  3. Joint Venture Agreements: Shared ownership and deeper cooperation.
  4. Consultancy Contracts: Advisory and technical services.
  5. Turnkey Project Agreements: Delivery of a fully operational facility.

c) Key Clauses and Conditions

Important clauses include:

  • Scope of technology
  • Duration
  • Geographical territory
  • Exclusivity
  • Payment terms
  • Royalty rates
  • Technical assistance
  • Training
  • Technical documentation
  • Performance guarantees
  • Quality standards
  • Reporting and monitoring
  • Audits

e) Confidentiality and IP Protection

Confidentiality clauses protect proprietary information, trade secrets and know-how from unauthorised disclosure.

IP clauses define ownership and permitted use of patents, trademarks and copyrights.

Strong IP protection encourages technology sharing and long-term cooperation.

f) Termination and Dispute Resolution

Agreements should define conditions for termination, such as breach of contract, non-payment or expiry.

Dispute resolution may involve arbitration, mediation or legal proceedings.

These mechanisms provide a structured way to resolve disagreements and minimise disruption.

7. Summary — For Exam

  • Technology transfer is the movement of technological knowledge, skills and technology from one entity to another for practical use.
  • It includes technical knowledge, management techniques, operational skills and physical equipment.
  • Effective transfer helps companies improve competitiveness and efficiency without spending excessively on independent R&D.
  • The process includes identifying the technology need, searching and selecting technology, evaluating feasibility, negotiating, implementing, absorbing, adapting and continuously learning.
  • Technology transfer is affected by technical, economic, organisational, human-resource, cultural and legal factors.
  • Traditional models generally view transfer as one-way, while modern models emphasise interaction, feedback, learning and adaptation.
  • Major transfer modes include licensing, joint ventures, FDI, strategic alliances, turnkey projects and consultancy.
  • Governance mechanisms and codes of conduct help ensure fairness, transparency, IP protection and sustainable transfer.
  • Governments support transfer through policies, public R&D institutions, technology parks, incubators, incentives and MSME/startup programmes.
  • Well-managed technology transfer can promote industrial development, innovation capability and long-term economic growth.

  3. Know-how: Practical technical knowledge required to operate or manage technology effectively.

  14. FDI: Investment by foreign firms that often brings technology and expertise.

9. Case Study — Indian Auto Parts Company

An Indian medium-sized auto-parts manufacturer wanted to reduce export rejection rates and improve quality.

A foreign business specialising in precision machining entered into a technology-transfer arrangement with the Indian company.

The Indian company received state-of-the-art CNC equipment, process manuals and technical training for operators and engineers.

Initially, productivity increased only slightly because staff were confused about the new software interface and maintenance processes.

Management responded by investing in supplementary training, forming interdisciplinary learning teams and adjusting processes.

The result was a 30% decrease in defects, increased production speed and acquisition of new export contracts within one year.

The company also began improving its own processes, increasing its internal innovation potential.

Q1. Factors Initially Limiting Success

  • Skill gaps: Staff were confused about the software interface and maintenance, showing insufficient technical know-how.
  • Weak absorptive capacity: Hardware was transferred without sufficient depth of training.
  • Adaptation problem: Technology was not initially customised to local workforce skills and workflows.
  • Human resource and cultural issues: Resistance, confusion and lack of interdisciplinary coordination.
  • Technical issues: Inadequate understanding of documentation and insufficient local support.

Q2. How Training and Organisational Changes Improved Absorption

  1. Assimilation: Supplementary training for operators and engineers built internal knowledge.
  2. Adaptation: Processes were adjusted to fit the new CNC technology, local materials and organisational needs.
  3. Organisational dimension: Interdisciplinary learning teams improved coordination, communication and knowledge sharing.
  4. Learning-oriented transfer: The company moved from passive use towards active learning, experimentation and continuous improvement.
  5. Effective utilisation: Defects decreased by 30% and production speed increased.
Key Lesson: Adoption alone is not enough. Effective technology transfer requires learning mechanisms, management commitment and organisational readiness.

Q3. Long-Term Strategic Benefits

  1. Improved productivity and efficiency, including a 30% reduction in defects.
  2. Enhanced product quality, reduced export rejection and new export contracts.
  3. Development of technical capabilities and reduced dependence on external experts.
  4. Enhanced innovation potential through improvement of internal processes.
  5. Long-term competitive advantage based on internal knowledge.
  6. Greater technology independence and self-reliance through stronger absorptive capacity.
Case Study Lesson: Technology transfer alone is insufficient. Absorption capacity, training, organisational learning, cooperation, adaptation and continual development are crucial for converting transferred technology into sustainable capability.
